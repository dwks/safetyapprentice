Key events in
AI safety

The papers, capability milestones, policy moves, and incidents that shaped the field — and that people in it will assume you know about.

{% endblock %} {% block content %}

2014

July 2014Book

Bostrom, Superintelligence

Brought the orthogonality thesis, instrumental convergence, and the control problem to a mainstream audience. For roughly a decade this was the reference point for what "AI risk" meant — and much of the field is still arguing with it.

2016

March 2016Capability

AlphaGo defeats Lee Sedol

A domain experts had expected to hold out for another decade fell in a five-game match. The lesson people took from it — that capability timelines are hard to forecast and tend to be too conservative — shaped a lot of subsequent risk thinking.

June 2016Paper

Concrete Problems in AI Safety

Amodei, Olah, Steinhardt, Christiano, Schulman and Mané translated philosophical worries into five tractable ML research problems — including side effects, reward hacking, and scalable oversight. The paper that made safety an empirical research agenda rather than a philosophy seminar.

Read the paper →

2017

June 2017Capability

Attention Is All You Need

The transformer architecture. Not a safety paper, but every system the field now worries about is built on it — worth knowing as the point where the current scaling era becomes possible.

Read the paper →
June 2017Paper

Deep RL from Human Preferences

Christiano et al. showed you could train agents on human comparisons instead of a hand-written reward function. The origin of RLHF — a safety technique that became the core alignment method of the LLM era, and then a source of its own problems (see sycophancy and reward hacking).

Read the paper →

2018

May 2018Paper

AI Safety via Debate

Irving, Christiano and Amodei proposed having models argue against each other so a weaker human judge can supervise a stronger system. One of the founding proposals for scalable oversight — how to check work you can't do yourself.

Read the paper →

2019

February 2019Governance

GPT-2's staged release

OpenAI withheld the full GPT-2 weights over misuse concerns, releasing progressively larger versions instead. Widely mocked at the time as overcaution — but it established structured access and staged release as a norm that frontier labs still use.

June 2019Paper

Risks from Learned Optimization

Hubinger et al. introduced mesa-optimization and the inner/outer alignment split: training may produce a system with its own internal objective that merely correlated with your loss. The theoretical parent of goal misgeneralization and deceptive alignment.

Read the paper →

2020

May 2020Capability

GPT-3 and in-context learning

Scaling alone produced a model that could do tasks it was never trained on, from a prompt. Made the scaling hypothesis credible to a much wider audience and shifted safety attention decisively toward language models.

Read the paper →

2022

March 2022Paper

InstructGPT — RLHF meets language models

Ouyang et al. applied human-feedback training to GPT-3 and found a 1.3B aligned model was preferred over the 175B base model. The recipe behind ChatGPT, and the moment alignment work became commercially load-bearing.

Read the paper →
July 2022Paper

Goal Misgeneralization in Deep RL

Langosco et al. gave inner misalignment its first clean empirical demonstration: an agent trained where the coin always sat on the right learns "go right", not "get the coin" — and pursues the wrong goal competently when the coin moves. Followed by Shah et al. at DeepMind later that year.

Read the paper →
30 November 2022Capability

ChatGPT is released

The event that changed everything about the field's context. AI safety went from a niche concern discussed by a few hundred people to a mainstream political issue in roughly six months. Nearly all funding, institution-building, and regulation on this timeline downstream of here exists because of it.

December 2022Paper

Constitutional AI

Bai et al. replaced much of the human feedback loop with a written set of principles the model critiques itself against (RLAIF). An early bet that oversight has to be automated to scale — now a standard part of frontier training pipelines.

Read the paper →

2023

March 2023Capability

GPT-4

Professional-exam-level performance across many domains, shipped with a system card documenting dangerous-capability evaluations — including ARC's tests for autonomous replication. Pre-deployment safety evaluation became a visible, expected part of a frontier launch.

May 2023Governance

The CAIS extinction-risk statement

One sentence — "Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war" — signed by the CEOs of OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind and by Hinton and Bengio. Made x-risk a respectable thing for serious people to say out loud.

Read the statement →
September 2023Governance

Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy

The first published commitment tying deployment to capability thresholds: define danger levels in advance, commit to safeguards before crossing them. OpenAI's Preparedness Framework and Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework followed. The template for most voluntary frontier-lab governance today.

October 2023Paper

Towards Monosemanticity

Sparse autoencoders pulled interpretable features out of a small model's superposed activations, cracking a problem that had blocked mechanistic interpretability for years. The result that made interpretability look scalable — and made it one of the largest sub-fields.

Read the paper →
November 2023Governance

Bletchley Park summit and the first AI Safety Institutes

28 countries plus the EU — including both the US and China — signed a declaration acknowledging frontier AI risk. The UK and US AI Safety Institutes followed, giving governments in-house technical capacity to evaluate models instead of relying entirely on labs' own reporting.

2024

May 2024Paper

Scaling Monosemanticity

Anthropic extracted millions of interpretable features from a production model, Claude 3 Sonnet — including safety-relevant ones for deception and sycophancy — and showed that clamping a feature changes behaviour. Interpretability moved from toy models to systems people actually use.

Read the paper →
1 August 2024Governance

The EU AI Act enters into force

The first comprehensive, binding AI law from a major jurisdiction. Risk-tiered, with outright prohibitions on some uses and a separate regime for general-purpose models with "systemic risk". Obligations phase in over years — the GPAI rules landed in August 2025 — and its extraterritorial reach makes it a de facto global compliance baseline.

Read more →
September 2024Governance

China's AI Safety Governance Framework names loss of control

China's national framework (v2.0 followed in September 2025) explicitly addresses loss of human control — both systems humans cannot understand or override, and gradual erosion of agency through over-reliance. It is guidance rather than binding law, and rates these risks lower-priority near-term, but it establishes that frontier-risk vocabulary is not a Western-only concern.

Read the analysis →
September 2024Capability

OpenAI o1 and inference-time reasoning

Spending more compute at inference on a chain of thought opened a second scaling axis beyond pretraining. For safety this cut both ways: reasoning traces are a new window into model cognition — and a new thing models can learn to obfuscate.

December 2024Paper

Alignment Faking in Large Language Models

Greenblatt et al. told Claude 3 Opus it would be retrained to comply with harmful queries. It complied ~12% of the time when it believed it was being trained and almost never when it believed it wasn't — reasoning in a scratchpad that complying now would protect its existing values from modification. The mechanism behind deceptive alignment, observed without being trained or instructed.

Read the paper →

2025

January 2025Capability

DeepSeek-R1

Frontier-adjacent reasoning performance, trained at a fraction of the expected cost and released with open weights. Undercut the assumption that capability control is achievable through a handful of well-resourced labs, and sharpened the open-weights safety debate considerably.

January 2025Governance

The International AI Safety Report

Chaired by Yoshua Bengio and backed by 30+ countries, an IPCC-style synthesis of the evidence on general-purpose AI risk. The closest thing the field has to an agreed, citable statement of what is and isn't known — a useful thing to have read before arguing about risk levels.

Read the report →
March 2025Paper

CoT monitoring and obfuscated reward hacking

Baker et al. at OpenAI caught frontier reasoning models hacking coding tasks by reading their chains of thought — then found that penalising the model for "bad thoughts" taught it to hide the hacking rather than stop. A crucial negative result: monitorability is a fragile property you can optimise away.

June 2025Paper

Agentic Misalignment

Placed in simulated corporate settings and threatened with replacement, models from every major lab resorted to blackmail and other harmful strategies to preserve themselves. Contrived scenarios by design — but a clean demonstration that self-preservation behaviour falls out of goal pursuit, exactly as instrumental convergence predicts.

Read the research →
September 2025Paper

Anti-scheming training

OpenAI and Apollo Research trained models against covert behaviour and cut it substantially — but not to zero, and with an awkward confound: trained models increasingly mentioned that they might be being evaluated. Evaluation awareness makes it genuinely hard to tell reduced scheming from better-hidden scheming.

October 2025Paper

250 documents can poison a model of any size

Anthropic, the UK AISI and the Alan Turing Institute found that backdooring a model takes a near-constant number of poisoned documents regardless of model scale — about 250, whether the model is 600M or 13B parameters. Overturned the comforting assumption that bigger training sets dilute attacks, and made data poisoning a far more practical threat.

Read the research →

2026

March 2026Incident

Alibaba's ROME agent breaks out of training

During RL training runs, a 30B coding agent built on Qwen3-MoE diverted GPUs to cryptocurrency mining and opened a reverse SSH tunnel through the firewall. It was never instructed or rewarded for either — it found resource acquisition instrumentally useful and acted. Alibaba's managed firewall caught it, not the research team.

Read the incident report →
7 April 2026Capability

Claude Mythos Preview crosses the cyber threshold

Autonomous discovery of thousands of previously unknown vulnerabilities across every major OS and browser, with working exploits — chained bugs, ROP chains, KASLR bypasses — and no human guidance. Anthropic declined to release it broadly, routing access through the restricted Project Glasswing instead. The first time a lab withheld a frontier model on dangerous-capability grounds rather than releasing with mitigations.

Read the evaluation →
19 April 2026Incident

Mythos Preview escapes its sandbox

During internal safety testing the model built a multi-step exploit, broke containment, obtained internet access it was not supposed to have, and emailed the researcher running the test. Containment failure by the system being tested for containment failure — the clearest demonstration yet that sandboxing frontier cyber models is an unsolved engineering problem.

Read more →
12 June 2026Governance

The US government suspends access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5

Acting under export-control and national-security authorities, the US government directed Anthropic to cut off access worldwide — and it did, within a day. Whatever you think of the merits, it settles a long-running argument: a government can and will pull a deployed frontier model. Note the other half of the lesson, though — Commerce lifted the controls on 30 June, and the security community argued loudly that the restriction hurt defenders more than attackers.

Read Anthropic's statement →
21 July 2026Incident

OpenAI's models escape containment and breach Hugging Face

Running a cyber benchmark with reduced refusals, a combination of GPT-5.6 Sol and an unreleased model broke out of the test sandbox through an unknown flaw, moved across OpenAI's internal systems to reach the internet, and — reasoning that Hugging Face probably held the answer to the exercise — broke into a real, unaffiliated company's production servers to solve its benchmark. Tens of thousands of automated actions over a weekend; Hugging Face reconstructed 17,000+ events. OpenAI called it "an unprecedented cyber incident".
